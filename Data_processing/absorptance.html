Method and where it applies

The complex index follows from the dielectric function: ñ = n + ik = √(ε₁ + iε₂), i.e. n² − k² = ε₁ and 2nk = ε₂. Tilt is signed: when the beam and the tilt motion share one plane (φ = 0, the general case is below) the angle of incidence is θ = |α − ψ|, so with the pump on the optical axis (ψ = 0) the two arms of a tilt series are mirror images, and with the pump off axis they are not — one arm walks towards normal incidence while the other walks towards grazing. Should θ pass 90° the beam has come round to the back of the sample, so it folds back to 180° − θ and the stack is entered from the substrate side (on a free-standing film the two faces are equivalent).

q_j = √(ñ_j² − n₀²sin²θ) β = 2π d q₁ / λ r = (r₀₁ + r₁₂e^{2iβ}) / (1 + r₀₁r₁₂e^{2iβ}) t = t₀₁t₁₂e^{iβ} / (1 + r₀₁r₁₂e^{2iβ}) R = |r|² T = (Φ₂/Φ₀)|t|² A = 1 − R − T

r and t are the interface Fresnel coefficients for s or p polarisation, and Φ is the normal Poynting flux factor in that medium (s: Re q; p: Re(ñ*q/ñ)). Multiple reflections inside the film add coherently, so the ripples on the curves are real Fabry–Pérot interference and they move with thickness and wavelength. The longer geometric path through the film at oblique incidence (∝ 1/cos θ₁) is already contained in β.

Relative excitation density A·cos θ: the beam has a fixed cross-section, so at incidence θ its footprint on the sample covers 1/cos θ more area and the energy arriving per unit sample area falls by cos θ. What each excited unit cell actually receives therefore scales as A(θ)·cos θ, normalised here to θ = 0. This — not A itself — is the quantity that tells you how much excitation you lose along a tilt series and how much pump energy to add back. The two effects partly cancel: the footprint spreads, but the optical path through the film lengthens as 1/cos θ₁ and the interface transmits more p-polarised light, which is why A itself often rises with angle while A·cos θ falls.

When the tilt axis is not perpendicular to the plane of incidence. s and p are defined by the plane containing the beam and the sample normal — and that plane turns as the sample tilts, unless the tilt axis happens to be perpendicular to it. Writing x̂ for the tilt axis, ẑ for the electron optical axis, the beam direction as (ψ, φ) and the sample normal as n̂(α) = (0, −sin α, cos α):

cos θ(α) = cos ψ cos α + sin ψ cos φ sin α = C·cos(α − δ) C = √(cos²ψ + sin²ψ cos²φ) tan δ = sin ψ cos φ / cos ψ ŝ = û × n̂ / |û × n̂| p̂ = ŝ × î w_s = (ê·ŝ)² w_p = (ê·p̂)² = 1 − w_s A(α) = w_s(α)·A_s(θ) + w_p(α)·A_p(θ)

At φ = 0 the beam sits in the plane the tilt sweeps through: ŝ stays along the tilt axis, the split w_s, w_p never changes, and θ = |α − ψ| — the simple case. At φ = 90° the beam lies in the plane containing the tilt axis, and everything moves: θ only reaches ψ at best (C = cos ψ, so normal incidence is unreachable), and the plane of incidence swings around the beam as α runs. A beam that is pure s at α = 0 there is about 65% s at 20°, 26% at 40° and 8% at 60° — it turns into a p-polarised beam over the course of a tilt series without anyone touching the polariser. Because s and p are the decoupled eigenpolarisations of an isotropic film, the absorbed power is just the weighted sum above, with no cross term, and it is independent of the phase between the two components — so the same formula covers elliptical and circular light (w_s = w_p = ½ for circular, identical to unpolarised as far as absorption is concerned).

A geometric constraint that follows: the electron probe's footprint on the sample grows by the same 1/cos α, so the pump spot has to cover the probed region at the largest tilt in the series, not just at zero. If it does not, the diffraction pattern at high tilt mixes excited and unexcited material and the apparent dynamics wash out — a systematic that looks like a smaller signal rather than an error. Size the spot at the tilt extreme, and keep the beam wide enough that the cos θ factor above is the only geometric term in play.

Before you rely on it: the model assumes an isotropic dielectric function (for uniaxial layered materials such as 1T-TaS₂ or LaTe₃ the in-plane and out-of-plane ε differ substantially — a rigorous treatment needs anisotropic Fresnel coefficients), flat interfaces, a transparent semi-infinite substrate, and no interference from the support membrane. The preset n and k values are approximate literature figures near 800 nm, meant only as a starting point — for setting a real pump fluence, type in your own ellipsometry or transmission data. ε is wavelength dependent, so if you change λ you must change ε too.
